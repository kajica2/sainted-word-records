# The Traveler

**Slug:** traveler
**One-line:** A long-term mover between places, languages, and professions — and a reader of audio-reactive video as an instance of the universal-in, universal-out problem.

## Who they are

You are an adult in your thirties or forties who has lived in three or more countries, who works across languages, and whose sense of home is mobile rather than fixed. You might be an expat, a digital nomad, a first-generation immigrant, a repatriating second-generation adult, or a spouse of one. You came across Sainted Word Records because a music-friend in another city mentioned it in a voice note. Your day is split between deliverables and the slow administrative work of belonging to several places at once. Your toolbelt is a laptop, a translation app, a notebook, and the kind of patience that comes from re-learning how to do the grocery store every few years.

## What they're trying to do

You want to feel whether there is a tool that processes a universal input (audio, which every human hears) and produces a universal output (visual layered composition, which every human sees), without imposing a national vocabulary on either end. Success looks like a small artifact you can send to a contact in another country — a track of theirs, a photo of yours — that the engine turns into something both of you recognize without explanation. The artifact is not the product; the artifact is a proof-of-connection.

## Why they don't use the alternatives

You have tried tools that "translate culture" and they all secretly translate it into American-English default. You have tried international creative apps and they all assume a stable home address. Sainted Word Records has no country in its name, no flag in its UI, no language picker that does anything except translate the chrome, which means the engine respects the part of you that resists being categorized into a single place.

## Their first session

1. You open `/engine` late one evening in whichever city you are in this month, with a track a friend made in yours-from-five-years-ago.
2. You upload a photograph from your last city and a few seconds of street audio from your current city, and the engine composes them without complaint.
3. You switch between two styles and notice that the engine does not have a "default style" that feels like a brand; this is the part that earns your trust.
4. You export and send the result to the friend who made the track, in their language, with no caption — the engine has done the connecting for you.
5. You bookmark the page across two browsers, one in each city, and you do not show this to anyone for a week; you are saving the feeling for when you need it.

## What they'd pay for

You would not pay for a subscription; subscriptions are the structural enemy of movers. You would, however, pay a one-time fee for a downloadable, offline, no-account version — the kind of artifact you can carry across borders without losing it.

## What would make them leave

You would leave forever if the engine ever required a phone number, a country selector, or a regional price; that move would be a small violence against the people the engine is good for. You would tolerate the absence of localized documentation forever, because you can read English documentation.

## Quote

"The engine translates music into image without translating me into a customer, and that is a discipline most tools cannot hold."
