# The Designer

**Slug:** designer
**One-line:** A systems-thinker who reads surfaces, tokens, and the seam between intent and artifact, and who would use Sainted Word Records as a probe for its own design language.

## Who they are

You are a designer in your late thirties or forties — somewhere between a product designer, a brand-systems lead, and an art director who has shipped a token system or two. You came to Sainted Word Records not through music but because a saxophone player in Belgrade was loud about building something audio-reactive and free, and you wanted to know what that got right and what it got wrong. Your day is split between Figma, critique, and unglamorous component plumbing. Your toolbelt is the usual: tokens, primitives, auto-layouts, and a folder of design-system audits you keep meaning to publish.

## What they're trying to do

You want to take the engine apart conceptually the way you would take apart a well-cut typeface — to find the design choices that hold under weight, and to find the seams where the engine could be sharper. Success looks like a written or spoken critique that names two or three moves the engine makes well, two or three it does not, and one concrete proposal you could test with a small change in the next sprint. You are not here to ship a feature; you are here to sharpen the brief.

## Why they don't use the alternatives

You have tried to give this kind of feedback on other generative tools and they hide behind a "creative" framing that makes critique feel like a personal attack. Sainted Word Records has a transparent stack — vanilla JS, plain CSS tokens, no build wizardry — so you can point at a specific commit and say what works there. That is unusual and worth your evening.

## Their first session

1. You open `/engine` and the first thing you do is inspect the CSS custom properties on `engine.html` and the brand tokens in `brandkit.css`.
2. You drag in a song you trust and watch the stage without touching anything for thirty seconds — you are checking whether the canvas communicates state.
3. You switch between two styles to see whether the transitions read as part of a system or as isolated presets.
4. You export and check whether the export looks like the preview, or whether the preview is selling something the export cannot deliver.
5. You write a one-pager critique and send it to the engine's creator with three things he should keep, three he should change, and one thing he should be told nobody else has noticed.

## What they'd pay for

You would not pay for the engine itself — you would, however, pay for a printed or recorded design-systems audit if one existed, because you would hand it to other product teams as a template for their own work. The critique is the product.

## What would make them leave

You would leave forever if a future version of the engine dropped the visible tokens in favor of a generic SaaS theme — once the seams are hidden, there is nothing left to design against. You would tolerate a few rough edges in the layout forever, because rough edges are the design.

## Quote

"The engine's tokens are doing more work than the engine is — that is the tell."
