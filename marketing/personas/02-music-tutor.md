# The Music Tutor

**Slug:** music-tutor
**One-line:** A long-form music teacher whose job is not to grade but to grow another human's relationship to sound, and who uses reactive visuals as a second voice in the lesson.

## Who they are

You are a piano or composition tutor who has been teaching one-on-one for fifteen-plus years, with a couple of certificate students and one or two competition-track teenagers. You found Sainted Word Records through a musician's group chat because someone said "it watches the audio," and that phrase caught you. Your day is four to six lessons, an hour of admin, and an evening of your own practice that is the actual reason you teach. Your toolbelt is your instrument, a pencil, a notebook that has become illegible, and a slow-growing tolerance for screen-based teaching tools.

## What they're trying to do

You want a tool that lets a student see their own playing, instead of only hearing it, because every beginner you have ever taught has improved faster the moment they got a mirror. Success looks like a student running an exercise on a tablet, hearing themselves, watching the visuals respond, and saying "oh — that is what an accent means." You do not want entertainment; you want a hand on the back of the head.

## Why they don't use the alternatives

You tried a few DAW-based visualizers for lesson use and they confuse the student because the visualizer is also a multitrack editor, and the lesson becomes a software lesson. You tried plain music-tutor apps and they gamify instead of reveal. Sainted Word Records watches the audio and shows the audio — no score, no shortcuts, just the relationship between sound and image — which means a student who is afraid of notation can still discuss phrasing.

## Their first session

1. You open `/engine` on the studio iPad before a Tuesday lesson, with the student due in fifteen minutes.
2. You drop in a phrase from a Bach invention and watch the engine read its shape — the moment of friction is that you almost immediately try to use this for notation, which it is not built for, so you put that thought down.
3. You play the phrase yourself into the room using the device mic as a test, and the engine approximates a tempo that is too quick to trust — you realize a microphone mode is a real gap.
4. You draw a lesson plan around two pieces: a Mozart sonata and an Etling, set the engine to `kinetic` style, and play during the lesson.
5. You assign the student to make a one-minute visual interpretation of the second phrase of the Mozart for next week.

## What they'd pay for

You would pay a yearly fee for a small "studio mode" that listens to a microphone and locks visuals to phrases, because you would buy that for any private teacher who asked you. You would not pay a per-student fee; the math does not work in your studio.

## What would make them leave

You would leave forever if a parent misread the visuals as "screen time" and you had to defend the lesson in an email. You would tolerate a slow load on older iPads forever, because your students already know the iPad is slow.

## Quote

"A reactive visual is a music tutor that does not get bored and does not interrupt."
