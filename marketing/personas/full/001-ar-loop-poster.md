# The AR Poster Maker

**Slug:** ar-loop-poster
**Surfaces touched (3+ minimum):** AR, recorder, library, presets
**One-line:** A zine maker who turns 5-second WebM clips into shareable markerless AR stickers that print on demand.

## Who they are
You are a graphic designer in your late twenties who moonlights as a risograph zine editor in a city with one working riso. Your day is split between paid identity work, two nights a week of print prep, and a slowly growing Patreon where readers send you reference images and ask for posters. Your toolbelt is InDesign, Illustrator, the riso software, and the phone camera you use to mock up placements.

## What they're trying to do
You want to drop a 5-second looped WebM of a poster into /ar-loop, watch it float in your studio, and email the link to a print client so they can see the poster as a real object in the world before approving the run. When the loop works, you want to capture it again, this time with a friend holding their phone, and post the result to your zine's Instagram. You do not need editing — you need a fast way to turn a flat asset into something a non-technical client can hold.

## Which surfaces they actually use, and why
1. **/ar-loop** — the entry point; drop a GIF you exported from your last poster, see it as a loopable object in space via the markerless AR scene.
2. **Recorder** — the 5-second WebM capture button is the only export you trust; you re-record from a phone every time you change the loop, not once and ship.
3. **Library** — you keep a private folder of 30 short loops and use the AR scene's file picker instead of the gallery because your files are not in the curated library.
4. **Presets** — when a loop needs to be brighter or warmer, you grab the `gallery` anchor preset at warmth=0.7 and let the FX pass push the colors before re-recording.

## A typical session (90-180 minutes)
1. You finish a poster for an upcoming show in InDesign and export a 480x480 WebM loop of the headline element spinning once.
2. You open /ar-loop on your phone (the desktop page tells you "Use a mobile device for live AR" — you already know).
3. You tap the file picker and pick the WebM, the A-Frame scene loads, and the loop appears on the floor of your studio at the marker origin.
4. You walk around the loop to confirm it reads from behind — the spin is symmetrical so it does. You record 5 seconds via the Record button, the WebM saves to your phone's camera roll.
5. You share the AR link with the print client via iMessage with "open this on your phone, point it at your desk" — the link is the share button's output.
6. The client replies two hours later that the poster is "too pink." You open the same WebM in /ar-loop again, this time with the `tape` preset's temperature slider at -0.2 to cool the loop, and re-record.
7. The client approves. You post the 5-second WebM to Instagram, then export the loop as a still frame for the printed zine cover.
8. You almost quit at step 3 — the first time you tried /ar-loop you did it on your laptop and got the "use a mobile device" hint. You almost dismissed it as broken until you realized the page is honest about being a phone-first surface.

## What they'd pay for
You would pay for a one-time purchase of "Pro" that unlocked higher WebM bitrate (so the riso dot pattern reads on close-up) and watermark removal on the share link. You would not pay a monthly fee — you make posters, you do not need subscriptions.

## What would make them leave
You would leave forever if the share link started requiring a login to view, because the point of sending the link is that the client can open it without committing to an account. You would tolerate the mobile-only AR constraint forever — you have a phone, and laptops in the AR scene are an honest limitation, not a bug.

## Quote
"I open the AR loop on my phone, the WebM records in five seconds, and the link works without a login — that's the only reason my printer ever approves anything."
