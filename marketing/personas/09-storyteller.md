# The Storyteller

**Slug:** storyteller
**One-line:** A narrator whose craft is arc, tension, and release — and who reads an audio-reactive engine as a way to feel whether a story breathes.

## Who they are

You are a working storyteller in your forties or fifties — a novelist, a folk-tale teller who runs oral-story residencies, a long-form podcast narrator, or the kind of adult who still gets asked to tell the story at a campfire. You came to Sainted Word Records sideways, through a friend who works in audio production and who mentioned the engine offhand. Your day is divided between solitary composition and room-based storytelling — a performance, a school visit, a workshop with teenagers. Your toolbelt is paper, your voice, a small recorder, and a deep skepticism of any tool that wants to "help you tell stories" because most of those tools do not understand what stories are.

## What they're trying to do

You want to understand whether there is a way to feel a story's architecture without listening to it — whether a listener's body could hear the *shape* of the arc in a visual layer running underneath the telling. Success looks like a workshop in which a participant says "I did not know a story could have a heartbeat until I watched this thing breathe under mine." You do not want content; you want a diagnostic.

## Why they don't use the alternatives

You have sat through a hundred "storytelling tools" and they are character builders, plot-point fillers, beat-sheet templates — not a single one of them helps you understand whether the **arc** is breathing. Sainted Word Records listens to the audio of your telling and lets the visuals rise and fall with it, which is the only thing a storyteller cannot fake in a written outline: the *length of a moment*. You want a tool that respects that length.

## Their first session

1. You record a six-minute telling of a folk tale you already know by heart and load it into `/engine` in private.
2. You watch the canvas without sound at first, and you notice which seconds the engine treats as loud — most of them are the seconds the room would lean in.
3. You play the recording back with the visuals running, and the moment of friction is that the engine's "BPM" does not map to your story's BPM the way your drummer-friend would mean it; you let the mismatch sit.
4. You try a `kinetic` style, then a `smoke` style, and notice that smoke softens the ups and downs in a way that may suit a lullaby story but not an origin story.
5. You write three sentences in your workshop journal: "If a visual breathes under a story, the writer can hear their own pacing without a mirror."

## What they'd pay for

You would not pay for software; you would, however, accept an invitation to write a one-page essay on the engine's relationship to oral storytelling for an anthology. That kind of credit feeds back into your practice.

## What would make them leave

You would leave forever if a future version of the engine added a "story arc" preset button that imposed a three-act template on the visuals — that is the move that would tell you the engine has decided to be about content instead of breath. You would tolerate any number of small latency issues forever, because stories have always had small latencies.

## Quote

"I do not need the engine to tell a story; I need the engine to tell me when my own story is breathing wrong."
