# The Multisystem Specialist

**Slug:** multisystem-specialist
**One-line:** A practitioner who crosses domains — sound + image + code + group process — and who reads the engine as a small instance of a larger pattern they have spent years mapping.

## Who they are

You are a working generalist in your late thirties or forties — a clinician, a designer-developer, a researcher who spans two or three fields. You came to Sainted Word Records because someone mentioned it in a thread about cross-domain tools, and the cross-domain framing interested you more than the music. Your day is split between a primary practice (therapy, education, design) and a side practice in another domain entirely, and those two halves have always needed a bridge. Your toolbelt is whatever each half requires, plus a notebook you have used since university to map the bridge.

## What they're trying to do

You want to know whether the engine is a real example of the pattern you study, or whether it is just a tool. Success looks like a one-page note you can add to your running map: "Sainted Word Records — audio-reactive composition — maps to pattern X if you remove the audio-reactive assumption." You are not here to use the engine; you are here to confirm or deny a hypothesis.

## Why they don't use the alternatives

You have tried many tools in this category and most of them are domain-specific in a way that prevents them from being examples — they are too obviously "music" or too obviously "video." Sainted Word Records has a thin layer of domain assumptions on top of a more general engine, which makes it legible as a pattern rather than as a product, and that is rare.

## Their first session

1. You open `/engine` with a sketchbook nearby.
2. You drop in a track and watch the canvas without any aesthetic judgment — you are looking at the architecture.
3. You open DevTools briefly to confirm the loadout matches what the about page claims, because claims about stack matter for a multisystem read.
4. You switch versions and notice where the engine can be reconfigured without breaking the seams.
5. You write two columns in the sketchbook: "what this engine shows," and "what this engine hides." That is the bridge note.

## What they'd pay for

You would not pay for the engine; you have seen enough tools. You would, however, be willing to be quoted in a future piece of documentation as a multisystem reader, because that kind of credit is worth more than a subscription.

## What would make them leave

You would leave forever if the engine ever lost its transparency — once a tool becomes opaque to its own architecture, it stops being legible as an example. You would tolerate whatever the engine does not do, because absence is information too.

## Quote

"The interesting move is what the engine refuses to do, and it refuses to do the obvious."
